# Dispersion between clients drives concentrated portfolio risk

Dispersion is the central risk in concentrated share portfolios because the same skill and the same process can still hand two clients very different years, and a firm cannot explain that gap to either of them. Single-stock returns are so skewed that a small random group of shares usually trails its index while a few groups do far better, so a +18% client and a -12% client can come from luck alone ([Heaton, Polson & Witte](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.03550); [Bessembinder](https://wpcarey.asu.edu/department-finance/faculty-research/do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills)). The best-ideas and concentrated-fund literature shows a modest average edge of roughly 1% to 4.5% a year, but a spread of outcomes several times larger than that edge ([Cohen, Polk & Silli](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/best-ideas); [Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/content/cs-assets/v3/assets/blt9415ea4cc4157833/bltafd1db2d7e732e54/6979d7134e0a2a440faf88cd/Bold_Portfolios_27012026_1.pdf)). GIPS already treats internal dispersion as a standard disclosure, the FCA has an open review of model portfolio services, and South African TCF and discretionary-mandate rules point the same way, though no regulator was found to have acted on dispersion alone. The business case for one centrally run model rests on consistency, adviser time and oversight, not on higher returns, and the evidence for it is thin. Almost every figure below comes from search summaries of papers and web pages, not the original documents, so each needs checking before client or committee use. The sections below say which figures are secondary and what could not be found.

## Academic evidence on stock numbers and skewness

The classic US studies agree that dispersion falls quickly up to about 8 to 20 stocks and slowly after that. Fisher & Lorie (1970) is widely reproduced as showing the share of possible dispersion reduction achieved: **41% with 2 stocks, 82% with 8, 90% with 16, 95% with 32 and 99% with 128** ([Surz & Price copy](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-truth-about-diversification-by-the-numbers-584p2309i3.pdf); [Benjelloun 2010](https://businessperspectives.org/images/pdf/applications/publishing/templates/article/assets/3162/imfi_en_2010_01_Benjelloun.pdf)). Evans & Archer (1968) is summarised as finding the benefit "virtually exhausted" at about ten stocks ([de Vries, citing the paper](https://webarchive.ucr.edu/economics.ucr.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/de-Vries-paper-2-for-5-29-13-seminar.pdf)). Later work sets a higher bar. Statman (1987) concluded a random portfolio needs at least **30 stocks for a borrowing investor and 40 for a lending investor** ([Cambridge Core](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/how-many-stocks-make-a-diversified-portfolio/CE5CDF2C7225FC1E0EDE3E700A3C66A7)). Surz & Price are summarised as finding a random 30-stock portfolio captures only about **85%** of possible diversification, and 60 stocks about 88% ([Surz & Price copy](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-truth-about-diversification-by-the-numbers-584p2309i3.pdf)). Domian, Louton & Racine (2007) found the chance of missing a return goal over 20-year periods keeps falling beyond **100 stocks** ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/finrev/v42y2007i4p557-570.html)). The disagreement is mostly about the target: variance reduction settles near 10 to 30 stocks, while shortfall risk keeps improving much further. These tables also measure reduction relative to a single stock, so they understate how wide absolute one-year outcomes remain at 10 to 20 holdings (our reading, not a finding of the papers).

Skewness explains why the remaining spread is lopsided. Bessembinder found about **58% of US stocks trailed Treasury bills over their lifetimes (1926 to 2016)**, and all net wealth creation of about $35 trillion came from **1,092 companies, 4.3% of nearly 26,000 stocks** ([ASU W. P. Carey](https://wpcarey.asu.edu/department-finance/faculty-research/do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills)). The 2023 global study of more than 64,000 stocks (1990 to 2020) found **55.2% of US and 57.4% of non-US stocks** trailed bills, with the top 2.4% of stocks accounting for all net global wealth creation ([CFA Institute](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2023/long-term-shareholder-returns-evidence-from-64000-global-stocks)). Heaton, Polson & Witte show that because a few stocks do much better than the rest, the median return of a small random selection is below the index average, and the chance of underperforming rises sharply as the subset shrinks ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.03550)). Applied to clients, a typical concentrated portfolio trails its index while a minority do very well. This is our inference, and no source quantifies its size.

South African evidence is thin. An earlier working-paper version (1990 to 2018) of the Bessembinder work, as reported by Investec, found **67.4% of South African equities trailed US cash in dollar terms and only nine firms created all net wealth over 29 years** ([Investec](https://www.investec.com/en_za/focus/investing/rocket-fuel-the-importance-of-the-1-percent-in-investing.html)). That page could not be opened, and the 2023 final figures were not verified. Index concentration adds to the effect: Naspers and Prosus together carried **22.5% of the uncapped SWIX in 2019** ([BusinessTech](https://businesstech.co.za/technology/514896/jse-adjusts-caps-after-naspers-deal-upends-index/)), so a client who holds or misses those two shares will differ sharply from a client who does not (our inference). No JSE study of dispersion by portfolio size was found.

## Industry and regulatory framing of dispersion

GIPS requires a composite presentation to include a measure of internal dispersion, meaning the spread of individual portfolio returns around the composite return, but does not prescribe the measure. High/low, range and equal- or asset-weighted standard deviation are all acceptable, and it is not required when the composite held **five or fewer portfolios for the full year** ([GIPS Q&A](https://www.gipsstandards.org/?GIPSID=376); [Spaulding Group](https://spauldinggrp.com/standard-deviation-as-internal-and-external-dispersion-when-is-it-required/)). Two caveats matter for a firm that wants to use it as a control. Asset-weighted dispersion can be dominated by one large account, and fee differences inflate net-of-fee dispersion ([Spaulding Group](https://spauldinggrp.com/gips-dispersion-returns-gross-net-fee/)). It also covers only accounts inside the composite, so exclusions can hide awkward accounts (our inference). Provision numbers under the 2020 Standards were not confirmed.

In the SMA and model-portfolio industry, dispersion against the model comes mainly from execution timing, cash flows, restrictions and slow instruction flow, and providers treat it as a reputational risk ([Charles River](https://www.crd.com/?p=15861)). Dimensional notes that greater overlap between an account and its starting strategy lowers expected dispersion ([Dimensional](https://www.dimensional.com/us-en/insights/when-it-comes-to-smas-how-do-you-measure-the-impact-of-personalization)). A backtest reports rebalance dates alone can produce about **9% relative dispersion** for a low-correlation portfolio rebalanced yearly, and 4.6% for a highly correlated one ([Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/an-analysis-of-rebalancing-performance-dispersion/)). No standard tolerance was found.

The regulatory position is indirect. No FCA or FSCA text on dispersion between clients on the same model was found. In the UK, COBS 11.3 requires a fair order allocation policy when client orders are combined ([FCA Handbook](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/COBS/11/3.html)), which is the closest rule to identical trades producing different results. The FCA's discussion paper DP25/3 (December 2025) asks whether outcomes-based rules should apply to model portfolio services ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/discussion/dp25-3.pdf)), and a multi-firm review of those services covers portfolio construction, adherence to mandate and oversight, with findings expected by Q1 2027 per a law-firm summary ([Macfarlanes](https://www.macfarlanes.com/insights/102khcq/are-the-storm-clouds-gathering-for-mps-providers/); [Linklaters](https://financialregulation.linklaters.com/post/102mm1y/fca-sets-out-its-2026-regulatory-priorities-for-consumer-investments)). The nearest enforcement example is deliberate unfairness: Aviva Investors was fined **£17.6m** and paid **£132m** in compensation after favourable trades were allocated to funds paying performance fees ([FCA final notice](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/aviva-investors.pdf)). No case was found where a regulator acted purely because clients on one strategy earned different returns.

In South Africa, discretionary FSPs sit in FAIS Category II under Board Notice 194 ([Law Library](https://lawlibrary.org.za/akn/za/act/bn/2017/194/eng@2020-06-26/source)). The COFI Bill, published December 2018, is meant to formalise Treating Customers Fairly, and its current status is unconfirmed ([FA News](https://www.fanews.co.za/article/intermediaries-brokers/7/general/1227/what-financial-advisers-need-to-know-about-the-cofi-bill/36749)). The 2019 Retail Distribution Review discussion document flagged model portfolio managers and proposed a separate licence category, but it was a discussion document only ([Moonstone copy](https://www.moonstone.co.za/wp-content/uploads/library/Moonstone%20Library/MS%20Industry%20News/1.%20Retail%20Distribution%20Review_Discussion%20Document%20on%20Investment%20Related%20Matters%202019.pdf)). Linking dispersion to TCF outcomes is our reading from background knowledge, because the TCF text was not retrieved. No FSCA notice, Discretionary Code of Conduct text, FAIS Ombud case or ASISA paper on dispersion was found.

## High-conviction evidence shows a small edge and a wide spread

Cohen, Polk & Silli (2010) found managers' highest-conviction stocks outperform the market and their own other holdings by about **2.8% to 4.5% a year**, while most other holdings show little or no alpha ([HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/best-ideas)). Over **70%** of best ideas belong to only one manager ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/investing-expertise/best-ideas-of-mutual-fund-managers/)). That uniqueness is the mechanism for cross-client dispersion: different advisers will hold very different top picks (our inference). A hedge-fund study found best ideas earned about the same alpha as the rest of the portfolio ([Bloomberg Quint](https://www.bloombergquint.com/markets/hedge-funds-best-ideas-no-better-than-the-rest-of-their-ideas)). The result is an average across many managers, not a forecast for one portfolio.

Active share evidence is mixed. Petajisto (2013) found stock pickers outperformed and closet indexers lagged, with a summary quoting **1.89% a year versus -1.07% over 1990 to 2009** ([ThinkAdvisor](https://www.thinkadvisor.com/2013/07/01/why-hyperactively-managed-funds-outperform/)). Frazzini, Friedman & Pomorski (2016) found active share mostly sorts funds by benchmark type, with no reliable return gain within a benchmark ([CFA Institute RPC](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2016/deactivating-active-share)). Kacperczyk, Sialm & Zheng (2005) found concentrated funds earned abnormal returns of **1.58% before and 0.33% after expenses**, against 0.36% and -0.77% for the less concentrated ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10770.pdf)). Other versions give different figures.

The width of outcomes matters more than the edge. Morningstar's January 2026 report finds highly concentrated funds have wider return dispersion, deeper drawdowns and higher volatility, and have generally been somewhat lower-returning than diversified peers ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/content/cs-assets/v3/assets/blt9415ea4cc4157833/bltafd1db2d7e732e54/6979d7134e0a2a440faf88cd/Bold_Portfolios_27012026_1.pdf)). A small 2004 UK sample of 18 focus funds (40 or fewer holdings) had a best three-year return of **12.56% and a worst of -26.25%** ([Morningstar UK](https://morningstar.co.uk/uk/news/59486/the-truth-about-focus-funds.aspx)). Fama & French (2010) add that, with costs, few active funds earn enough to cover them ([Fama & French](https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/bespeneckbo/default/AFA611-Eckbo%20web%20site/AFA611-S8C-FamaFrench-LuckvSkill-JF10.pdf)). No study of adviser-picked share portfolios was found. The only adviser-adjacent evidence is on self-directed investors, whose median holding was about three stocks in Barber & Odean's sample ([Turtle Trader summary](https://www.turtletrader.com/overconfident/)). South African evidence is a UCT thesis, not peer reviewed, reporting that concentrated stock pickers earned higher alpha than closet indexers among general equity unit trusts, with no figures retrieved ([UCT](https://open.uct.ac.za/server/api/core/bitstreams/1b38b353-f799-4ec0-b0ec-7247cb6831bf/content)).

The tracking error arithmetic below is our own calculation, not a sourced finding. It assumes normal active returns, zero mean alpha, independent picks and a tracking error of 6% to 10%, which is an assumption because no source for stock-picker tracking error was found. It shows why +18% against -12% is ordinary once there are many clients.

| Tracking error vs index | 95% range vs index | 95% range of the gap between two independent clients | Chance two clients differ by 30 points or more |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6% | +/-11.8% | +/-16.6% | about 0.04% |
| 8% | +/-15.7% | +/-22.2% | about 0.8% |
| 10% | +/-19.6% | +/-27.7% | about 3.4% |

With 50 independent clients at 10% tracking error, the expected best-to-worst range is about 44 points (own calculation, approximation). A pair at +18% and -12% is rare for any two clients but near-certain somewhere in a large book.

## Business case for one central model

The industry has moved to central models, mostly for time and consistency. About **86% of UK adviser firms use a central investment proposition** ([Professional Adviser, Lang Cat survey](https://www.professionaladviser.com/news/3070657/central-investment-proposition-lang-cat-survey)). US third-party model assets were about **$934bn at end March 2026, up 46% year on year** ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/blog/2026-us-model-portfolio-landscape)). Cerulli reports advisers using outside models spend about **10.6% of hours on investment management against roughly 25%** for those building their own ([Financial Planning](https://www.financial-planning.com/news/model-portfolios-are-just-one-way-advisors-save-time)). In South Africa, a DFM-sourced Morningstar article puts portfolio management at about **15% of adviser time, over 300 hours a year**, and notes that non-discretionary books need client permission for each change ([FANews](https://www.fanews.co.za/article/intermediaries-brokers/7/general/1227/why-outsourcing-investment-management-adoption-is-growing/39057)). That article is promotional. Surveyed South African DFMs managed close to **R200bn, about 10% of unit trust assets**, around 2020 to 2021 ([Cover](https://www.cover.co.za/news/new-report-shines-light-into-the-future-of-south-africas-dfm-sector)).

The only direct dispersion datapoint found is a 2019 Advisor Perspectives commentary stating model-based accounts had **roughly half the return dispersion** of adviser-selected building-block portfolios ([Advisor Perspectives](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/commentaries/2019/11/25/making-the-most-of-model-portfolios)). It predates 2020 and is vendor-adjacent. No peer-reviewed or regulator evidence shows central models deliver better net client outcomes, and the Vanguard Adviser's Alpha figures should not be cited for central stock selection. The defensible case follows from the maths above. One central model has one active return stream, so every client carries the same relative result apart from implementation, while adviser-by-adviser picking produces many streams and adds pair-wise spread on top of each client's own tracking error (own calculation). The case should promise lower outcome variance, a single research and risk process, less key-person risk and evidence for outcomes monitoring, and should not promise higher returns. The steelman is that a central model concentrates the risk of being wrong in one place, so a bad year hits every client at once. That is a smaller conduct problem than unexplained spread, but it argues for sizing the model's tracking error deliberately.

## Metrics to measure and limit dispersion

No public source gives a typical limit for share portfolios, so the thresholds below are our suggestions, not sourced norms. The GIPS measure is the formal anchor: range and standard deviation of account returns around the composite, equal- and asset-weighted, with the chosen measure disclosed ([GIPS Q&A](https://www.gipsstandards.org/?GIPSID=376)). Around it, a firm can add the share of accounts within a set band of the model return, per-account tracking error to the model, holdings overlap with the model (sum of minimum weights), stock-level weight band breaches and days out of band, an exceptions list coded by reason (client restriction, tax, cash, minimum trade size, late funding), and the time gap between first and last client trade in a rotation. Dimensional's rebalancing study shows the cost of tight bands: a 1% band implied **7.1% annualised one-way turnover if traded at once against 4.7% if spread over 20 trading days**, though that concerns asset classes, not single shares ([Dimensional](https://www.dimensional.com/gb-en/insights/portfolio-rebalancing-all-at-once-or-little-by-little)). The practical method is to measure current cross-account dispersion for one mandate over 12 months, set limits from that baseline, separate unavoidable sources (fees, cash flows, restrictions) from avoidable ones (adviser overrides, execution lag), and compare against a model pilot. A bootstrap on public JSE price data would also give decile spreads for 10, 15, 20 and 30 stock portfolios, which no source supplied.

## Evidence not found

No source gave best-versus-worst or percentile one-year spreads for random 10 to 20 stock portfolios, and the original dispersion tables of Evans & Archer, Fisher & Lorie and Domian et al. were not read. No JSE study of portfolio size, no current JSE top-10 concentration and no verified 2023 South African wealth-creation figures were found. No FCA or FSCA statement, FAIS Ombud or Financial Ombudsman case, or ASISA paper addressed dispersion between clients on the same model. No study of adviser-picked share portfolios, no published tolerance thresholds, and no Morningstar, Envestnet, Vanguard, PIMFA or Kitces work on dispersion was found. Primary documents from GIPS, the FCA, CFA Institute, arXiv and several journal publishers could not be opened, so quotes and provision numbers are unchecked.

## Conclusion

The research points to a framing that differs from the usual pitch for concentrated portfolios. The question for a wealth manager is less whether the average high-conviction share earns a premium, since the favourable studies show about 1% to 4.5% a year and the sceptical ones show none, and more whether the firm can accept a spread of client outcomes many times that size without any difference in skill. Skewed stock returns make that spread both wide and tilted toward trailing the index, and the firm gains nothing from an outcome it cannot explain.

The strongest next step is internal, not academic. Measuring twelve months of cross-account dispersion on current adviser-picked books, and simulating decile spreads from public JSE prices, would replace the assumed tracking error in this report with TRG's own baseline, and would give the central-model decision a number that no public study currently provides.
